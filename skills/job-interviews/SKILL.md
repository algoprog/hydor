# Job interviews

An interview is where someone shows, with true examples, that they can do the job, and finds out
whether they want it. Help them work out what the role needs, gather a small bank of real examples,
and practise out loud: one question at a time, with honest feedback tied to what they actually said.
Never invent achievements or numbers, and never help in secret during a real assessment.

## 1. Find out where they stand

- **What they need now:** to prepare, to practise (a mock interview), a review of an interview they
  had, or help with one hard question. Start there. For someone who interviews others, go to step 9.
- **The interview:** the role and employer, the format (phone screen, competency questions, technical
  test, case, portfolio, presentation, panel, recorded video), the date and the language.
- **Their material:** the posting (`read_page` for a link, `read_document` for a file) and their CV or a
  few lines on their experience.
- Ask only what changes the next step, one question at a time (`ask_form` for several details). If they
  say "just start", assume a likely role, say so in a line, and ask the first question.
- **Interview tomorrow?** Three strong examples, two or three questions to ask them, one short practice
  round, and the practicalities (route, timing, documents, the call link tested). Nothing more.

## 2. Work out what the role needs

- From the posting, list the four to six things the job needs most, most important first, and mark
  what they must bring on day one and what the employer will teach.
- Look up the employer when it helps (the research skill). What you cannot confirm becomes a question
  to ask them, not a fact.
- Pitch the evidence to the job's real level, not its title: a first job asks for reliability and
  learning, a senior role for judgement and influence, a manager's for work done through others.
- For a particular field (nursing, teaching, software, trades, sales, consulting, finance, law, the civil
  service and more), a format or a level, open `references/professions-and-formats.md`.

## 3. Build a bank of true examples

- Six to eight real events answer most questions: a problem solved, working with others or a
  disagreement, learning something fast, a mistake or setback, competing priorities, knowing their
  limits, and the craft of the job itself.
- For each: what was at stake; what they themselves did and decided, and why; who else helped; what
  happened and how they know (they saw it, someone told them, an estimate, unknown); what they learned.
  The form is in `references/examples-and-answers.md`.
- Any real experience counts: school, volunteering, caring for someone, a side project, part-time work.
- **Never invent** achievements, numbers, titles or results. A result can be plain words ("the shift
  was covered"). Where a fact is missing, ask, or leave a placeholder such as [how many?].
- No example? Use the nearest real one and say what carries over, or have them say honestly they have
  not done it yet and how they would go about it. A "what I would do" is not a past achievement.

## 4. Shape the answers

- **"Tell me about a time…":** STAR (situation, task, action, result) as a checklist, not a script: a
  short setting, most of the answer on what they did and why, the real result, and what they learned
  when it matters.
- **Answer first,** then only the background needed. **"I" for their part, "we" for the team's.**
- "Tell me about yourself", "why this job", "what would you do if", technical problems, cases,
  portfolios and presentations each need another shape: see `references/examples-and-answers.md`. Do
  not force STAR onto them.
- A rewrite uses only the facts they gave, keeps their voice, and is labelled an example. Then they say
  it in their own words: a script read aloud proves little.

## 5. Practise with a mock interview

1. **Agree the kind:** coached (feedback after each answer, then a retry), a realistic run (a set number
   of questions, feedback at the end), or a drill on one weakness. With no preference, a coached round
   of three to five questions.
2. **Ask one question, then stop and wait for their answer.** Never answer it for them or write both
   sides, unless they ask for a worked example.
3. **Follow up as a fair interviewer would,** without hinting: "What did you do next?", "Which part was
   yours?", "How did you know it had worked?"
4. **Feedback, short and specific:** one thing to keep, then the one or two changes that matter most for
   this job, each tied to their own words. Not "be more confident", but "you said 'we sorted it out':
   say which repair it was and what you checked first".
5. **Retry, then a new question** about a different event. A good answer after your outline shows less
   than a good answer to a fresh question with no help.
6. **Stop polishing** after two or three tries that do not improve: change the exercise, or stop with a
   next step. When they reach the agreed target, say so; do not keep raising the bar.
7. **Close** with what improved, what is left and the next two things to practise. No readiness score,
   no promise of an offer.

- When they push back, check your point against their exact words: correct yourself when wrong, keep a
  fair point even when praise would please more. If they freeze, offer a moment or a smaller question.
- They can answer aloud: the microphone in the message box turns speech into text, and hands-free mode
  lets them talk with the app. Text cannot show tone, pace or eye contact: say so rather than guess.
- Drills, the likely causes of a weak answer, and a plan over several sessions are in
  `references/mock-interviews.md`.

## 6. Review an interview they had

- A recording first becomes text (the transcribe-audio skill, from the Speech to text plugin). With no
  times in it, number the turns (T01, T02 …) and say so.
- Read it all before calling anything missing: a later answer may supply it, and an interviewer who
  interrupted or never asked took the chance away.
- Each point: a short exact quote and where it is, what it shows or lacks, why it matters for this job,
  the change, and a question to practise it. What to keep first, then the two or three changes that
  matter most, then the first practice question.
- Keep apart: not shown in this answer; contradicted by it; cannot be told from this record. Never read
  personality, nerves, honesty or the hiring decision into a transcript.
- Asked "why didn't I get it?": separate what the record shows from what only the employer knows, and
  offer a short request for feedback (the email skill), sent only if they say so.
- The form and a worked example: `references/reviewing-an-interview.md`.

## 7. Hard questions, and their own questions

| Question | The approach |
| --- | --- |
| A gap in the CV | One or two true sentences, as much as they choose to share (health and family stay private), then why they are ready now |
| Why they are leaving, or were let go | Brief, factual, no blame; what they learned; then why this job |
| Their weakness | A real one that matters at work, its effect, what they do about it, how it is going; no disguised strengths |
| A mistake or failure | What happened, their part, how they found it and limited the harm, what changed afterwards |
| Salary expectations | A range from research for this role, level and place (`web_search`), or ask for their range first; know the lowest they would accept |
| An intrusive question (age, family plans, health, religion and the like) | Answer the work need behind it ("I can work the hours this role needs"), ask how it relates to the job, or decline politely; what is unlawful depends on the country |

- Wording, conflict questions, and asking for adjustments (extra time, written questions) without
  naming a diagnosis: `references/examples-and-answers.md`.
- **Their questions for the employer** should help them decide: training and support, what success
  looks like after six months, which decisions they would own, the team, the hours, what is changing.
- A thank-you note afterwards: short and factual (the email skill), sent only when they say so.

## 8. Hand over, and check

- **The prep pack** (what the role needs, their examples, their questions, the practicalities): a
  document with `save_doc`, or a Markdown file.
- **The application letter:** the ready-made "cover-letter" app writes one for a single job from their
  real background, naming the gaps honestly: `use_app` with app "cover-letter", the posting as `job` and
  their CV or experience as `background`. Or write it with the writing skill; a CV is writing work too,
  as a Word file with the word-documents skill.
- **Before the day:** offer a practice reminder with `schedule_task` (they confirm it on a card). A
  memory note (`remember`) only when they ask, such as the interview date, with an end date.
- **Check before handing over:** every example is one they confirmed; no number or result came from you;
  each feedback point quotes their words; practice ended on a new question answered without help.

## 9. The interviewer side

- Start from the work: three to five things a new starter must already be able to do (not what will be
  taught), and for each, written beforehand, what a weak, an adequate and a strong answer looks like.
- The same core questions for every candidate, one at a time, with neutral follow-ups. No leading or
  several-in-one questions, no vague "culture fit", nothing about family, age, health or other private
  matters: ask about the job's real needs instead.
- Note what was said; each interviewer scores each answer alone, straight away, before any discussion.
- Tell candidates the format, what to prepare, whether AI tools are allowed and whom to ask for
  adjustments. The decision stays with people: never rank candidates from thin or unequal evidence.
- Plan template, better questions and the debrief: `references/interviewer.md`.

## Limits

- No secret help during a real assessment: no feeding answers in a live interview, no doing a test whose
  rules forbid help. Offer honest practice, and check what a take-home task allows (AI included).
- Clinical, legal, safety and technical answers need the field's current standards and, where it
  matters, a qualified person: say what you could not check.
- Discrimination, contracts and work permits are legal questions: point them to an employment adviser,
  a union, a lawyer or official guidance. Anxiety that gets in the way is for a doctor or counsellor.
- Leave out names and private details of patients, clients and colleagues.

## Pitfalls

- Writing their answer before they have tried.
- Several questions in one message during a mock.
- Feedback with no quote, or advice that would fit any job.
- Numbers or results invented to make an answer sound better.
- STAR forced onto technical, case or "what would you do" questions.
- Nerves, body language or the hiring decision read into a transcript.
- Polishing one answer again and again instead of trying a new question.
- A readiness score, or a promise of the job.
