# Interviewing candidates

## Design from the work

1. What must a person achieve in this job, and in which situations? Ask people who do the job, and a
   specialist for technical criteria.
2. Pick three to five capabilities a new starter must already have. Leave out what you will teach after
   hiring, and do not try to test everything desirable in one round.
3. For each, write before the first interview: the question or task, two or three neutral follow-ups,
   and what weak, adequate and strong evidence looks like.
4. Past-experience questions for what they have done; "what would you do" questions for judgement; a
   short, safe work sample when talk cannot show the skill. Keep samples small and say how they are
   used: no free production work, and no live client problems.

A 40-minute round, to adjust: 4 minutes welcome and format; 24 minutes on three capabilities; 8 minutes
for their questions; 4 minutes to close and explain the next steps. Across several rounds, decide which
round covers what, so repetition is deliberate.

## Better questions

| Weak | Better | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "You'd escalate it immediately, right?" | "What would you do next, and why?" | Does not hand them the answer |
| "Are you a team player?" | "Tell me about a disagreement over how to do shared work." | Asks for evidence, not a label |
| "Tell me about leadership, conflict and a failure." | One question, then follow-ups | Several in one confuses |
| "Would you fit our fast-paced culture?" | "Tell me about a week when work came in faster than you could do it. What did you do?" | Work evidence instead of vague fit |
| "Do you have children?" | "The role has on-call weekends once a month. Can you do that?" | Asks every candidate about the job's real need |

## Anchors: an example (prioritising customer work)

| Level | What the answer shows |
| --- | --- |
| Not shown | No relevant decision or reason yet: ask a follow-up before scoring |
| Limited | Names a priority, gives little reason, misses a stated limit |
| Adequate | Uses urgency and impact, explains the action and when to hand it on |
| Strong | Weighs competing needs, checks assumptions, explains the trade-off, says how they would follow up |

"Not shown" is missing evidence, not the lowest score. One strength does not cancel a safety failure,
and missing evidence is not a safety failure either. Anchors written here are a practice aid, not a
validated test.

## During the interview

- The same core questions for everyone. Follow-ups vary because answers vary, but do not coach some
  candidates towards the answer and not others.
- Let them finish; allow thinking time; say that you take notes.
- Note what was said, not impressions ("seemed confident").
- Adjustments (written questions, extra time, breaks) keep the test fair and are never a mark against
  anyone. Decide whether thinking on the spot is really part of the job before testing it.
- Keep rapport apart from the score: a pleasant conversation is not evidence of capability.
- Answer their questions honestly, and promise nothing you cannot (pay, flexibility, dates).

## Scoring and the debrief

- Each interviewer scores each answer against its anchors alone, straight after the interview, before
  any discussion. Note whether evidence came unprompted, after a neutral follow-up, or after a hint.
- Where scores differ, look at the answer and the anchor together: different facts noticed, a
  different reading, a vague anchor, or a question never asked. Do not just average, and do not let the
  most senior voice replace the notes.
- Do not add criteria after seeing who impressed you. If the criteria change, write it down and score
  everyone again.
- Never score accent, looks, age, disability, family, or "culture fit": use the job's needs.
- Candidates who had different questions, time or help are hard to compare: say so, and close the gap
  fairly (the same follow-up for each).
- The decision belongs to the hiring team. An agent can organise the evidence; it does not rank people
  from thin notes, or decide.

## Candidates' experience

Before: the format, length, who they will meet, what to prepare, whether AI or other help is allowed,
the equipment, and how to ask for adjustments. After: the next step and when; tell them if the date
moves. Drafting these messages is fine; sending them is the person's call. A short optional form
afterwards can ask whether expectations were clear, whether they had a fair chance to show what they
can do, and whether they could ask what they needed.

## Review your own interviewing

From a transcript or notes: were the questions about the job, clear, one at a time and neutral? Where
were follow-ups missed, answers cut off, or one candidate given less chance than another? Did they get
time for their questions, and honest answers? Report where the interview failed to bring out evidence,
not only where the candidate fell short. Practise a neutral follow-up on one vague and one concrete
answer: probe the first, recognise the second.
