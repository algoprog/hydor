# Examples and answers

## The example bank

One row per real event. Fill in only what is true; leave [?] where a fact is missing, and ask.

| Event (when, where) | What it shows (which needs of the job) | What was at stake | What they did and decided, and why | Who else helped | What happened, and how they know | What they learned or changed | Keep private |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

- **How they know a result:** they saw it; someone told them; it is an estimate; unknown. The answer
  says which ("the lead told me later that the shift was covered").
- **A balanced bank:** a problem solved; working with others or a disagreement; learning something
  fast; a mistake or setback; competing priorities; knowing their limits or doing the right thing; the
  craft of the job. Not everyone needs a revenue figure, a team they led or a crisis.
- **One event, several questions:** fine, as long as the facts they pick answer the question asked. One
  memorised story for every question is not.
- **Little experience:** school, volunteering, caring, clubs, part-time work and projects count. An
  apprentice is not expected to show what a qualified practitioner would.
- **No example at all:** say so, and show how they would learn or approach it. A "what I would do"
  answer stays labelled as that, never turned into a story that did not happen.
- Missing facts become questions or placeholders. A modest example is never stretched into a claim of
  leadership.

## Shapes by question

| Question | Shape | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Tell me about yourself" | What they do now, the experience most relevant to this job, why this job next; one to two minutes, then invite questions | The life story, or the whole CV read out |
| "Why this job?" "Why us?" | What they genuinely like in the work, what in their experience fits, what they have checked about the employer | Invented lifelong passion, flattery, unchecked claims about the employer |
| "Tell me about a time…" | STAR: the situation in a sentence or two, their task, what they did and why (most of the answer), the result and how they know, what they learned | A team story with no "I"; a success story when the question asked about a failure |
| "What would you do if…" | What they would find out first, what matters most, what they would do within their role, what they would do if it went wrong; assumptions said aloud | Turning it into a past event that did not happen |
| A technical problem | Clarify what is asked, the approach, do it, check it (examples, edge cases), its limits | A fluent explanation in place of a checked answer |
| A case (a business problem) | Clarify the decision, a structure made for this case, sums with units, a recommendation with its main risk and the next check | A memorised framework that does not fit the problem |
| A portfolio piece | The brief, their own part, the choices and what they rejected, the feedback and what changed, the outcome or why it is unknown | Claiming the team's work; impact nobody measured |
| A presentation or a lesson | Who it is for, one goal, the evidence, how they check the audience understood; timed | Polished slides standing in for content |

The structure should make their reasoning visible; its labels are not what gets marked. A clear, correct
answer that never names STAR is fine.

### Weak and stronger (made up: never put these facts into someone's answer)

- Weak: "I improved communication." Stronger: "I noticed the handover missed bookings still waiting
  for a callback, so I suggested a shared list of pending items. My colleague kept it up on the next
  shift, and we could see which customers still needed a call. We didn't measure waiting times." A
  specific action, shared credit, an honest limit.
- Weak opening: "I was born in… then I studied… then I worked…". Stronger: "I coordinate volunteer
  rotas, where I learned to fill gaps at short notice. I'm applying for this scheduling job because
  accurate handovers and helping people are what I want to do more of."
- Weak: "I'm a perfectionist." Stronger: "I used to give long explanations when people asked a simple
  question. A colleague told me it was hard to find the answer, so now I give the answer first and ask
  whether they want the detail. I still slip when I'm nervous."

## Hard questions in more detail

**A gap in the CV.** One or two true sentences, at the depth they choose ("I was caring for a family
member", "I took time out for my health and I'm fully ready now", "I was travelling and learning
Spanish"), then what kept them current and why they are ready now. They owe nobody medical or family
details.

**Why they are leaving, or why they left.** Short and forward-looking: what they want next that this
job offers. No criticism of the old employer. Dismissed: say so plainly in a sentence, what they
learned, then move on. Made redundant: the role went, not the person; say so. When the facts are
disputed, help them describe their side accurately; legal questions go to an adviser.

**A weakness.** A real one that affects work, what it has caused, what they are doing about it, and
how it is going, including what is still hard. Not "I work too hard". Not a skill central to the job
unless they can show honestly how they manage it.

**A mistake or failure.** What happened, their own part (no hiding behind "we"), how it was noticed,
what they did to limit the harm and whom they told, what they changed so it would not happen again. If
nothing could be put right, what they learned.

**A conflict.** The other person's view, fairly put; how they worked it out; the outcome; what they
would do the same or differently. A disagreement does not prove the other person was difficult.

**Salary expectations.** Beforehand, look up the range for this role, level and place (job ads that
show pay, salary surveys, official statistics) and settle three figures: what they would be glad to
get, what they will ask for, and the lowest they would accept. In the interview they can ask first
("What range have you set for this role?"), give a researched range with their target near its top,
or say they would like to understand the whole package first. Never lie about current pay; in some
places employers may not ask for it, so check locally. Weighing offers later: the whole package (pay,
holidays, hours, days at home, training), with the decision-brief skill.

**Intrusive or unlawful questions** (age, marriage or children, pregnancy, health or disability,
religion, nationality or where they are "really" from, sexual orientation, union membership). Options,
from the mildest: answer the work need behind it ("I can work the hours this role needs, including
some evenings"); ask how it relates to the job; decline politely ("I'd rather keep that private, but
I'm glad to talk about…"); raise it afterwards with the recruiter or HR. Which questions are unlawful,
and what can be done, depends on the country: say so, and for a complaint point to the official body or
an employment adviser. No answer is guaranteed to have no consequence; the choice is theirs.

**Adjustments for the interview** (extra time, questions in writing, breaks, a quiet room, another
format). A short, practical request: what they need and how it helps them show what they can do. A
diagnosis is not needed unless they want to share it; what employers may ask varies by country.

**"Do you have any questions for us?"** Always yes: two or three from the table below.

## Questions for the employer

| Situation | A useful question |
| --- | --- |
| First job | How are new starters trained, and who helps when they get stuck? |
| Shifts or frontline work | How are rotas set, and how are changes handled? |
| Trade, care or other regulated work | What supervision and equipment are there, and when should I stop and ask? |
| Specialist | Which decisions would I own, and which go to someone for review? |
| Manager | Which results am I accountable for, and what can I change (budget, hiring)? |
| Senior leader | Where do the board, the sponsor and the team see this job differently? |
| Any | What would make someone successful here after six months? What is changing in the team this year? |

Questions are for getting what they need to decide, not for showing off. Do not invent facts about the
employer: a worry is a question until there is evidence.

## Afterwards

A thank-you note the same or the next day: two or three sentences, something specific they enjoyed
discussing, and a fact they forgot to mention if it matters. Draft it with the email skill; it is sent
only when they say so. It can clarify an answer, but it is not a second interview.
