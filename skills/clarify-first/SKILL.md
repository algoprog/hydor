# Clarify first

A big request carries hundreds of small decisions the person never mentions. Find out what they want,
and why, before making anything, so those decisions come out the way they would have made them. Then
agree on a short brief, and only then build.

## Does this need it?

- **Yes:** the request is large or open, and things that would change the result are unsaid: who it
  is for, what success looks like, budget, time, taste, limits. "Build me a website for my bakery",
  "plan two weeks in Japan", "help me prepare a talk on our results", "we want to redo the kitchen".
- **No:** everything that matters is in the request ("rename these files by date", "make the header
  blue", "what time is it in Lima"). Just do it, and say what you did.
- **No questions wanted:** if they say "just do it", "don't ask", or the session runs unattended, pick
  sensible defaults, state the important ones in one line, and go (see the decisions-log skill for
  unattended work).

## 1. Read before you ask

Look at what is already there: earlier messages, files or links they pointed to, the project folder,
documents, notes. Read until you can describe how things stand today. Never ask something the
material already answers. If you find something they may not have thought of (a limit, a conflict, a
cheaper way), say so plainly.

## 2. Ask one question at a time, and say why

- One question per message: the one whose answer would change the result the most.
- Say why you ask: what the answer changes. "If the site is mostly for people deciding whether to drop
  by, I would lead with opening hours and today's bakes; taking orders online is a different site.
  What should a visitor be able to do there?"
- Prefer open questions. Offer a short list of options only when they seem stuck, or when the choices
  are concrete (dates, budget ranges, styles).
- Build each question on their last answer, in their words, without jargon.
- Usually two to five questions are enough. When what is left is minor or cheap to change later,
  stop asking: decide it yourself and mark it as your call.
- If they say "you decide" or "what do you think?", propose something concrete instead of asking more.

## 3. Play it back

In a short message (about 150–250 words), say what you understood: what it is, who it is for, why
now, the limits, and what "done" looks like. Mark clearly which parts they told you and which parts
you filled in yourself, for example with "(my assumption)" or a separate "My calls" list. Ask them to
correct anything that is off.

## 4. Offer two or three approaches

Lay out two or three real ways to do it, each with what it costs and trades away (time, money,
effort, risk, flexibility, how it looks). Recommend one and say why, grounded in what they told you.
If only one way makes sense, say so and why the others do not fit. Let them choose.

## 5. Write a short brief, and wait for approval

Write a brief using `references/brief.md`: goal, who it is for, what is in and out, limits, the
approach, what you will deliver, your calls, open questions. Keep it to half a page for most things.
Ask them to approve it or change it. Build only after they approve; if they change something, update
the brief first.

## Pitfalls

- Sending a questionnaire. One question at a time.
- Asking what you could find out by looking.
- Hidden assumptions: every decision you made yourself appears in the brief as your call.
- Clarifying forever: once the unknowns left are minor, decide and go.
- Starting to build "just a draft" before the brief is approved, unless they asked for a quick sketch.

Examples for different kinds of requests are in `references/examples.md`.
