# Brainstorming

Many ideas first, then a few good ones. Go wide with methods that push past the obvious, without
judging anything; then narrow on purpose, against criteria agreed before scoring. Keep the two phases
apart: judging while generating kills the unusual ideas, and they are the reason to brainstorm.

## 1. Frame the challenge

- Turn the topic into a question that starts "How might we …?", with who it is for and the limits.
  "How might we bring more regulars into the café on weekday afternoons, without extra staff?"
- Note the constraints (budget, time, brand, what must be avoided) and what a good idea has to do.
  Keep them for narrowing down; do not use them to filter yet.
- Ask what the person has already thought of and rejected, and why: the reasons show criteria they
  have not said.
- Set a target: 30 to 60 raw ideas for a real problem, 15 to 30 for a name or a title.

## 2. Go wide

Use three to five methods, not one: each finds ideas the others miss. Label each batch with its
method, so the person sees where the ideas came from. Prompts and a worked example for each are in
`references/methods.md`.

- **Obvious first:** write down the ten ideas anyone would have, to clear them out of the way.
- **Reversal:** how could we make the problem worse? Then turn each answer around.
- **Worst idea:** deliberately bad ideas, then find the useful part of each.
- **Constraints:** what if there were no budget? Only one day? It had to work for children? Ten
  times the customers? No website?
- **SCAMPER**, on something that exists: Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify (bigger or smaller), Put
  to another use, Eliminate, Reverse or rearrange.
- **Analogies:** how does another field solve this? A hospital, a game, an airline, a library,
  nature.
- **Other eyes:** a child, an expert, a competitor, a sceptic, a customer in a hurry, someone from
  another country.
- **Random input:** a random word or object, forced into a connection with the problem.
- **Combinations:** list the parts of a solution (who, what, where, when, how it is sold) with
  several options each, and mix across them.
- **Crazy eights** (for a person or a group): eight sketches or variations in eight minutes. For
  you: eight quick variations on the strongest idea so far.

While going wide:

- No criticism, your own included. Build on ideas ("yes, and …").
- Concrete over vague: "a stamp card that makes the third afternoon coffee free", not "loyalty".
- Keep about one idea in five wild: wild ideas often hold the seed of a workable one.
- Check your list for sameness. If most ideas are discounts, or all of them are digital, push into
  the areas not covered yet.

## 3. Narrow down

1. Merge duplicates and group the rest into five to eight themes; give each theme a name.
2. Drop ideas that break a must-have, with a word on why.
3. Score what is left on two to four criteria agreed with the person (impact, cost or effort, speed
   to try, fit). A 1-to-5 scale is enough. Or place them on impact against effort: **quick wins**
   (high impact, low effort), **big bets** (high, high), **fill-ins** (low, low), and **money pits**
   (low impact, high effort), which go.
4. Pick about three: one safe, one bold, one cheap to test. In a group, a vote with three dots each
   works.
5. Strengthen each pick: what would make it better, and the strongest objection with an answer.

## 4. Turn picks into tries

For each pick: the smallest real test, what it costs, how long it runs, and the result that would
mean go or stop. "Two weeks of a Wednesday study table with free refills; carry on if afternoon visits
rise by ten."

## 5. Present

- The question and its limits, in one line.
- The long list grouped by theme, one line per idea, with its method; compact.
- The short list with scores and reasons, then the picks with their tests.
- With many ideas, a board helps: a page with `show_html`, ideas as cards in the page kit's
  `.n-grid`, each with `data-choice`, so the person clicks favourites and they come back as their
  message.

## Names

For a business, product or project: agree the criteria first (says or suggests what it is, easy to
say and spell, memorable, unlike competitors' names). Generate by families: descriptive, invented,
compound, metaphor, place or founder, short or abbreviated. Then check the short list: the web
address and social handles (`web_search`), the same name already used in the field, unfortunate
meanings in the main languages of the market. A trademark search is a legal step: point to the
official registers, and to a lawyer for anything that matters.

## Pitfalls

- Judging while generating, out loud or by quietly leaving ideas out.
- Stopping at the first good idea.
- Every idea from the same angle.
- Ideas too vague to try.
- Fifty ideas and no recommendation.
- Scores treated as exact: they sort ideas, they do not prove anything.
