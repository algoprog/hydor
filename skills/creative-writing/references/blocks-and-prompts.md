# Blocks, prompts and habits

Open this when a writer is stuck, wants prompts or exercises, or wants a writing routine that lasts.

## Name the block before offering a fix

Ask what happens when they sit down to write, and where the story is, then match the fix.

| What it looks like | Likely cause | What helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Will not start; plans and re-plans; rewrites page one | Fear of writing badly (perfectionism) | Permission to write badly: a timed 15 minutes, a "rough" draft that nobody will see, the smallest next scene |
| Stops near the end; avoids finishing or sending | Fear of being judged, or of it being over | Name it kindly; finish the next scene only; share a small piece with one friendly reader |
| Wants to, but cannot focus; tired | Energy, sleep, too much else | A shorter session at a better time of day; five minutes counts; rest is part of the work |
| Does not know what happens next | Thin material | A what-if chain; ask what the character wants right now and what they will do about it; a prompt for this point of the story |
| The middle stalls; keeps going back to the start | The story's structure | Check the turning points; outline what exists; push forward and fix the start later |
| Polishing sentences while the plot is broken | Fixing the wrong thing | Structure first: sentences that may be cut do not need polish |
| "Thinking about it" for months | Avoidance | A small daily minimum (100 to 300 words) at a set time, kept for two weeks |
| Grief, depression, anxiety, a crisis | Not a writing problem | Stop coaching; be kind; suggest someone qualified, or a helpline if they are in danger. Writing can wait. |

Many writers lose heart about three-quarters of the way through a draft, as their characters do:
say it is common, and that the doubt says nothing about the book.

## Prompts that move this story forward

A prompt is worth having only if it adds to the writer's own draft. Before writing one, ask where
the story is (which quarter, which turning point is next) and what the character wants now.
Then give:

1. **One element** this point of the story needs (below).
2. **A concrete task** that continues their draft: a scene, with who is in it and what must change.
3. **A constraint** that forces fresh writing: no naming emotions; start mid-action; one sense
   besides sight; under 500 words.

| Where the story is | Elements that suit it |
| --- | --- |
| Beginning | The goal made concrete; what she stands to lose; the opponent's first move; the flaw shown without explanation; the point of no return |
| Middle | A small win that costs more than it gains; the opponent at full strength; a chain of three scenes, each causing the next; the middle turn, chosen at a cost |
| Crisis | Everything taken, in the worst order; failing at the very thing the climax will need; the past revealed at last |
| Ending | The changed character's decisive act, moment by moment; the return to a changed world; which loose end to leave open |

Example, for a story at its lowest point: "Write the scene where Mara learns the harbour board knew
about the boat all along. Tom is with her. By the end she must see her own part in what happened.
Do not name a single feeling; let us see them in what she does with her hands."

## Exercises for when a scene will not come

- **Twenty versions:** list twenty ways the scene could go. The first five are the obvious ones;
  the useful ones start around ten.
- **Three doors:** write the opening of the scene three times, starting at three different moments;
  keep the latest one that still makes sense.
- **Interview:** ask the character three questions and let them answer at length (what they would
  never admit, what they want tonight, what they are afraid you will make them do).
- **Swap the chair:** write the scene from the other person's point of view, then go back.
- **Letter to a friend:** for a pitch, synopsis or blurb, first write a plain letter to a friend
  about the book, then edit the letter into the document. The natural voice survives.
- **Ten-minute run:** write without stopping on "what she wants right now is..." and keep going.

Exercises warm up; they are not the work. Bring whatever they produce back into the draft.

## Habits that last

- **A floor and a target:** a minimum that still counts on a bad day (say 200 words) and a goal for
  good days (say 1,000). Missing the target is fine; keep the floor.
- **Do the sums:** words still to write ÷ writing days before the deadline = words a day.
  An 80,000-word novel at 500 words a day, five days a week, takes about 32 weeks.
- **Same time, same place** when they can; writing before email and messages.
- **Stop in the middle** of a scene, even a sentence, with a two-line note on what comes next:
  tomorrow starts easily.
- **A gaps list** instead of research mid-draft: `[to check: the harbour master's name]`, all
  looked up later in one go.
- **Track sessions, not moods:** a `save_page` tracker (date, words, minutes, where in the story) if
  they want one; a gentle reminder at their writing time with `schedule_task` if they ask.
- **Read like a writer:** a book they admire, twice; the second time list its scenes and turning
  points to see how it was built.
- **Company:** a small group of writers who give honest, kind notes, or one reading partner.
