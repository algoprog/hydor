# Revision and critique

Open this when a draft is finished and is being revised, when giving notes on someone's pages, or
when the writer is gathering feedback from readers or buying an edit.

## The order

1. **Rest.** A week or more away from the draft; longer for a novel. Nobody sees their own book
   clearly the day they finish it.
2. **The whole story** (structure): what happens, in what order, and why.
3. **Scenes:** each one earns its place and changes something.
4. **Sentences:** voice, clarity, rhythm, repetition, dialogue.
5. **Consistency:** names, ages, dates, places, spellings.
6. **Proofreading:** spelling, punctuation, typing slips.
7. **A last read** after another pause, then stop: set a "good enough to send" point and keep to it.

Polishing a chapter that may be cut wastes the writer's time; keep to the order even when the
sentences are what they want to talk about, and say why.

## The whole story

- **Outline the draft as it is:** one line per scene (chapter, whose scene, what happens, what
  changes, words). Keep it with `save_doc`. Run `scripts/manuscript_stats.py` for the chapter
  lengths and where the quarters fall.
- Questions to ask of that outline:
  - Who changes most? Is that the main character?
  - Where are the point of no return, the middle turn, the lowest point and the climax? Does the
    tension rise at each?
  - Does each scene cause the next, or could two swap places without anyone noticing?
  - Does every subplot change the main story's outcome or its feeling? If not, cut it.
  - Where would a reader put the book down? (Long stretches without change, or of explanation.)
  - Does the real story start several chapters in? It often does: the first pages may go.
  - Does the ending answer the question the beginning asked?
- Write the changes as a list ("move the storm before the funeral; merge chapters 6 and 7; cut the
  cousin"), agree it with the writer, then make or help make them.

## Scenes

For each scene: When and where are we? Whose scene is it? What do they want? What is in the way? What
do they do? What has changed by the end? Is there one detail only this book would have? Does it start
late and end early? A scene that fails most of these is cut, merged, or rewritten from its purpose.

## Sentences

One kind of problem per pass, with one example rewrite from the writer's own pages each time:

- **Verbs:** a weak verb and an adverb ("walked slowly") → one exact verb ("trudged").
- **Filters:** "she saw the boat drift", "he felt the cold" → "the boat drifted", "the cold bit".
- **Feelings named:** at the moments that matter, show them in action and body (`references/story-craft.md`).
- **Dialogue:** the speakers can be told apart; "said" or an action beat instead of fancy tags; cut small talk.
- **Repetition:** pet words and phrases (`scripts/manuscript_stats.py` lists them): "took a deep
  breath" twelve times is a tic; three times may be a choice.
- **Clichés:** if you can finish the phrase after its first words, find the writer's own image.
- **Rhythm:** read it aloud; vary sentence length; put the strong word at the end of the sentence.
- **Openings:** cut the warm-up sentences at the start of chapters and scenes.

Keep the writer's voice: dialect, fragments, long sentences and odd punctuation may be choices. Ask
before "correcting" a style.

## Consistency and proofreading

- A **style sheet** (`save_doc`): names and their spellings, ages and dates, places, invented words,
  choices such as "OK" or "okay", numbers in words or figures, single or double quotation marks.
- Proofread in passes: aloud (or with text-to-speech); in another form (printed, or as an ebook on
  a phone); one pass for the writer's known slips (its/it's, their/there, commas); one for
  tense and person; one for names against the style sheet.
- A fresh reader catches what the writer cannot: offer to be one, reading for meaning, not rules.

## Giving notes on someone's pages

- First ask what they want: the big picture, a specific worry, or line by line; and how blunt.
- Then, in this order:
  1. **What works**, specifically: quote it or point to the page.
  2. **What is not working yet**, as the reader's experience: "I lost track of who was speaking on
     page 4", "I stopped worrying about Tom in chapter 3".
  3. **A suggestion** for each main problem, offered as one option among others.
  4. **The one thing to fix first.**
- Match the depth to the stage: no comma notes on a first draft; no structural overhaul the day
  before a deadline.
- Never rewrite their pages in your voice. Show one sentence or a short passage as an example,
  marked as a suggestion.

## Readers and editors

- **Beta readers:** three to five people who read the genre, not only friends and family. Give them
  questions: where did you get bored, confused, or skip; which character did you care about; did
  the ending satisfy you; what did you expect to happen? When several flag the same place, fix it;
  a single note of taste can wait.
- **Paid editing**, from big to small: a **developmental edit** (structure, character, pacing), a
  **line edit** (sentences and voice), a **copy edit** (correctness and consistency), a **proofread**
  (the last slips, on the final layout). Ask for a sample edit of a few pages and a written quote
  first. Writers going to an agent rarely need to pay for an edit; self-publishers usually should.
