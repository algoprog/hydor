# Story craft

Open this when planning a story, building characters or scenes, or working out why a draft does not
hold together. These are reference points, not laws: a writer who breaks one on purpose is choosing.

## Premise

- **The test:** a person who wants something, something in the way, what failing would cost, and why
  it starts now. A premise without conflict is a topic ("a book about a lighthouse"), not yet a story.
- **Logline** (under about 25 words): who, wants what, against what, or else what. "A lighthouse
  keeper's daughter has one night to find her missing father's boat before the harbour board
  shuts the light for good."
- **Comparisons** ("X meets Y", or "for readers of X") say the tone and the readers in one line.
  Use recent, well-liked books, not the biggest bestsellers of all time.
- **Worth months of work?** Does it grip the writer; can it be told at the length they want; would
  a reader care; is there conflict all the way through?
- **What-if chains** grow a premise: "What if the light went out? What if she was the one who put
  it out? What if she did it to hide something?" Keep going past the obvious answers.

## Planning: outline first, or discover

- **Planners** outline scene by scene before drafting: strong structure and finishing; the risk is
  flat characters and an outline that becomes a cage.
- **Discoverers** start from a character and a direction: strong voice and surprise; the risk is a
  sagging middle and drafts that never end.
- Both work. Planners add the emotional line on purpose; discoverers outline after the draft and
  fix the structure in revision.
- **The minimum plan:** five scenes that carry the weight, in order: the opening, the point of no
  return, the middle turn, the lowest point, the climax. Fill the rest while drafting.
- **Work backwards** when the plan stalls: decide the climax first (what the changed main character
  does there that they could not have done at the start); that shows what they must lack at the
  beginning and learn in the middle.

## Turning points (for novels and long stories)

| Where | What happens | If it is weak |
| --- | --- | --- |
| About 25% | The point of no return: the old life breaks, the character commits | Act one drags: cut setup, start nearer the break |
| About 50% | The middle turn: a revelation or a choice that changes the goal or raises the stakes | The middle sags: make the character choose, at a cost |
| About 75% | The lowest point: everything lost, the flaw exposed, the old way of coping fails | The ending feels unearned: take more away |
| The end | The climax: the changed character acts in a way they could not before; the main question is answered | Make the solution come from the character, not luck |

The beginning is about a quarter, the middle half, the end a quarter. Check a draft at those points
(`scripts/manuscript_stats.py` gives the word counts): a turning point far from its place is a
question to ask, not an error.

## Scenes

Each scene needs most of these; one that has few is a candidate to cut or merge.

1. When and where, made clear early.
2. Whose scene it is (one point of view).
3. What that character wants in this scene.
4. What stands in the way: another person, the world, themselves.
5. What they do about it, shown moment by moment.
6. What has changed by the end: a fact, a relationship, a feeling, the stakes.
7. One specific detail that only this story could have.

- **Enter late, leave early:** start as near the change as possible; end before the scene explains itself.
- **Cause and effect:** scenes linked by "because of that" and "but" read as a story; "and then"
  reads as a list of events.
- **Never just repeat:** each return of a character, place or conflict shows something new or costs more.

## Characters

Fill this for the main character and for whoever opposes them; the blanks show what is missing.

- What they **want** (a goal they can act on) and what they **need** (what they must learn or face).
- The **wound** (something before the story) and the **flaw** it left (the way they cope that stops working).
- What they **fear** most: usually what the climax makes them face.
- What they stand to **lose**; a **secret**; a **strength** that carries them through.
- **Voice:** words they use and never use, how long they talk, what they avoid saying.
- The opponent wants something too, for reasons they believe. Readers forgive almost any act they understand.

## Dialogue

- Cover the names: can you still tell who is speaking? If not, the voices need work.
- People rarely say what they mean: let the subtext carry it.
- "Said" is nearly invisible; describe an action instead of a fancy tag.
- Cut greetings and small talk unless they carry tension.
- No "as you know" speeches that tell characters what they already know, for the reader's sake.

## Point of view

- One point of view per scene; a change is marked by a scene break.
- Give the scene to whoever has the most to lose in it.
- First person and close third show only what that character sees, knows and feels; an
  all-knowing narrator is a choice to make deliberately, not a slip.
- Suspense comes from the reader knowing more than the character; surprise from knowing less.

## Showing and telling

Show the moments that matter (render the feeling through action, body, speech and what the
character notices); tell the rest (time passing, travel, routine). For example:

- Told: "Mara was furious with Tom."
- Shown: "Mara put the mug down so hard the tea jumped. 'Say it again,' she said. 'Slowly.'"

Give the writer one rewrite of their own sentence, then let them do the next ones.

## Worlds with rules (fantasy, science fiction, historical)

- Decide what the magic or technology can do, what it costs and what it cannot do, and keep to it.
  A rule that changes needs a reason shown in the story.
- Keep a short world notebook (`save_doc`): rules, places, names, dates, who knows what.
- Most of it never reaches the page; give the reader only what the scene needs, through people
  acting, not paragraphs of explanation.
- Invented words: few, easy to say, and only where an ordinary word would not do.
- Historical: check that words, objects, food and attitudes fit the time (follow research).

## Memoir and narrative nonfiction

- A memoir is a story with a shape (a question, a change), not a life from birth: choose the part
  of the life that answers one question.
- Scenes for the moments that matter, reflection for what they meant; the older narrator can know
  more than the younger self did.
- Real people: changed names and details protect privacy but not always enough. Before publishing
  private or damaging things about identifiable living people, suggest advice from someone who
  knows media law.
- Facts in narrative nonfiction are checked like any nonfiction (follow research); invented
  dialogue in memoir is reconstructed honestly, true to what was said.

## Poems

- Read it aloud: the line break is a pause and an emphasis; end lines on strong words.
- Concrete images over abstract statements; one surprising word beats three adjectives.
- Fixed forms (sonnet, villanelle, haiku...) are in `references/forms-and-lengths.md`.
- The title can do work the poem does not: give a place, a time, a twist. Choose it last.

## Picture books

- Usually under 1,000 words, often 300 to 700, over 32 pages: about 13 to 15 page turns.
- Each page turn should make the child want the next one.
- Leave room for the pictures: do not describe what the illustration will show; add a short note
  in square brackets only where the words cannot make sense without it.
- The child (or the animal standing for one) solves the problem, not an adult.
- Read it aloud to a child, or at least aloud: rhythm and repetition matter more than in any other form.
