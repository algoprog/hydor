# Hard emails

Shapes, not scripts: change the words to fit the person and the thread, and keep the shape. Each
example is short on purpose; most hard emails get worse as they get longer.

## Saying no

Shape: thanks → the no → (one reason) → (a real alternative) → a warm close.

> Thank you for thinking of me for the panel. I can't take it on this spring: I'm committed to the
> launch until June. If you run another in the autumn, I'd be glad to be asked again.

- "I can't" or "I won't be able to", not "I'm not sure I can", which invites a second request.
- One reason at most. "I'm at capacity until June" is enough; the whole calendar is not.
- Offer an alternative only if you would be glad to give it: a later date, a smaller part, someone
  better placed (ask them first).
- **A job offer:** thanks, the decision, a kind word about the people you met, the door left open.
- **A supplier or a candidate:** decide quickly, say so plainly, thank them for their time. Give a
  reason only when it helps them and you are comfortable having it in writing.

Wording that is kind without blurring the answer: "I'm going to pass on this one." "This isn't one I
can take on." "It's a no from us this time, and here's why."

## Chasing

Shape: the request again in one line → what you need and by when → make it easy.

First chaser, after three to five working days:

> Subject: Re: Signed contract for the Lisbon venue
>
> Hi Marta, a quick nudge on the signed contract: we need it by Friday 14 Nov to hold the date. I've
> attached it again. If anything in it is a problem, tell me and we'll talk it through.

Second chaser, a week later, shorter and firmer:

> Hi Marta, we still need the signed contract to hold 6 March. Could you send it by Wednesday? If
> plans have changed, a one-line reply is fine and I'll release the date.

Closing the loop:

> Hi Marta, I haven't heard back, so I'll take it that March doesn't work and release the date on
> Friday. Do get in touch if you'd like to pick it up again.

- Never "just checking in" or "per my last email" on its own: restate the ask.
- Change something each time: a deadline, an easier option, another channel (a call), or another
  person (a colleague of theirs, with the first person in Cc).

## Apologising

Shape: what happened → what it did to them → ownership → what you did → what changes.

> I'm sorry: your order shipped a week late because we mislabelled it in our warehouse. I know you
> needed it for the 12th. We've refunded the delivery charge, and from this week every parcel is
> scanned before it leaves, so labels are checked twice.

- Apologise once, early, for the specific thing.
- Avoid "if any inconvenience was caused", "mistakes were made", "we apologise for any confusion".
- Do not blame a named colleague or supplier to a customer.
- When the mistake has legal or financial consequences (a data leak, a missed contractual date), the
  user should check the wording with whoever handles that before it goes.

## Bad news

> I'm afraid the kitchen won't be finished by 20 December. The worktop supplier has moved delivery to
> 6 January, which means about two more weeks without a sink. We'll set up a temporary one by Monday,
> and I'll confirm the new finish date as soon as the worktop ships.

- Lead with it: the reader will hunt for it anyway.
- Then what it means for them, and what you are doing about it.

## Replying to an angry message

> Thank you for telling me, and I'm sorry the repair failed again. You're right that we said it would
> be fixed on the first visit. Here is where things stand: … I'd like to call you tomorrow morning to
> agree the next step. Would 9:30 or 11:00 suit you?

- Acknowledge what is fair in the complaint before anything else.
- Answer their points in their order: facts, not adjectives.
- No sarcasm, no "as previously stated", no matching their tone.
- Suggest the user waits an hour, or a night, before sending.

## Asking a stranger or a busy person

> Subject: One question about your 2024 survey of bakery margins
>
> Dear Dr Okafor, I run a small bakery in Leeds and found your survey of bakery margins very useful.
> May I ask one thing: did the 18% average include the owners' own wages? A one-line answer would
> help a great deal. If you're too busy, no reply needed, and thank you for the survey itself.

- Who you are in one line, then the ask; "why them" in a phrase.
- Small and specific, with a way out.

## Declining or pushing back on a request from a manager

> I can take on the supplier review, but not before the 30th without moving the budget report. Which
> would you like first?

- Name the trade-off and let them choose; it is a no to the impossible, not to the person.

## Subject lines

- The topic and the action: "Venue decision needed by 14 Nov", "Q3 report attached, two questions",
  "Running 10 minutes late".
- A one-line message can live in the subject alone, ending "(EOM)" for "end of message", but only
  where the reader knows the convention.

## Words that make emails worse

| Instead of | Write |
| --- | --- |
| Per my last email | As I mentioned on Monday, … (and say it again) |
| Please be advised that | (cut it, and just say the thing) |
| Sorry to bother you | (cut it, or "Thanks for looking at this") |
| I was just wondering if maybe | Could you |
| ASAP | by Thursday at 5pm |
| Going forward | From now on (or cut it) |
| Kindly revert | Please reply |
| Do not hesitate to contact me | Just reply if anything is unclear |
