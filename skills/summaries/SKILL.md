# Summaries

A short summary the user hands in or sends as their own is judged on whether it shows the source was
understood: its point, why it holds, and, when asked, how it ties to what the reader already knows.

- Keep to the length asked: "three or four sentences" means four at most, about 100 words, so count
  them before you send. To fit, drop a detail rather than the point or a link that was asked for.
- Start with the subject and its claim (or "This article explains why…"), not with the author's name,
  the date or "argues".
- Give about half to what the source shows and why, in your own words, and half to the link that was
  asked for.
- Keep each detail's job: an example the source gives as a warning stays a warning. A number earns its
  place only if it carries the point.
- Make it one line of thought, each sentence building on the one before; one or two links made well
  beat four touched on.
- Link a course idea by applying it to a case in the source (this bond, this patient, this scene), not
  by defining it: the teacher knows the definition, and using it is what shows understanding.
- Use the field's terms exactly, without explaining them, when the reader knows the field. Commit to
  claims rather than hedging them ("can contribute to").
- Hand it over ready to use: one paragraph, with no heading, label or bold, and nothing before it. If
  you do not know what the course has covered, link what the source shows most directly, and say so
  in one line after the paragraph.
