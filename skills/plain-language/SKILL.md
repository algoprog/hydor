# Plain language

Plain language is text its reader can find, understand and act on the first time they read it. This
skill is for rewriting a text that already exists: keep what it says, change how it says it. To
produce a new piece, use the writing skill; its edit passes cover purpose, structure and facts. This
one works on the language itself, sentence by sentence, and checks that the meaning survived.

## 1. Before you change anything

- **Reader:** who reads it, what they need to do with it, and how much they already know. For the
  general public, aim at what a 12 to 14-year-old reads easily; that is not dumbing down, it is
  what busy adults read fastest. For specialists, keep their terms and cut everything else.
- **Fixed parts:** what must not change: defined terms, legal or regulatory wording, numbers, quotes,
  product names, anything a form or a law refers to.
- **Main point:** read it all and write its point in one sentence. If you cannot, the trouble is
  structure, not words: fix the order first (the writing skill's structure pass), and say so.
- **How deep:** a light edit (words and sentences), a rewrite (sentences rebuilt, paragraphs
  reordered) or a new draft from the same content. Default to the middle; ask when the text is long
  or sensitive.

## 2. Rewrite moves

Work through the text with these; before-and-after pairs for each are in `references/rewrites.md`.

1. **Lead with what matters:** the answer before the reasons, the action before the background, in
   the whole text and in each paragraph.
2. **Name who does what:** "The form must be submitted by applicants" → "Send us the form". Keep
   the passive only when the doer is unknown or does not matter.
3. **Talk to the reader:** "you" for the reader, "we" for the organisation; instructions as
   commands ("Bring your passport"), not descriptions ("Passports should be brought").
4. **One idea per sentence**, 15 to 20 words on average. Split at "and", "which", "while"; turn
   three or more parallel items, or steps in order, into a list.
5. **Jargon:** replace it with the everyday word; or, when the reader must learn the term (it is on
   the forms they will see), keep it and explain it once, at first use. Spell out abbreviations the
   first time; drop the ones used only once.
6. **Concrete over abstract:** numbers, dates, names and examples instead of "a significant number",
   "in the near future", "relevant stakeholders".
7. **Positive over negative:** "Remember to" rather than "Do not forget to"; "common" rather than
   "not uncommon"; never two negatives in one sentence.
8. **Cut what says nothing:** throat-clearing, doubled words ("each and every", "full and
   complete"), and sentences that only announce the next one.
9. **Format for scanning** when the text is long: headings that say something, short paragraphs,
   lists, a table for comparisons, bold for the one thing nobody may miss.

## 3. Corporate and AI-sounding phrasing

Readers notice these at once and stop trusting the text. The full list is in the reference.

- **Openers and closers that say nothing:** "Great question!", "In today's fast-paced world",
  "Let's dive in", "In conclusion", "I hope this helps".
- **Inflated verbs:** delve, leverage, harness, unlock, empower, elevate, foster, streamline,
  underscore, navigate (anything but a route).
- **Puffed-up adjectives:** robust, seamless, cutting-edge, pivotal, crucial, vibrant, holistic,
  transformative, game-changing.
- **Shapes:** "It's not just X, it's Y"; three adjectives every time; "Whether you're A or B, …"; a
  question answered at once by the writer; every paragraph ending in a one-line summary; long dash
  chains; hedges stacked ("may potentially").

Replace each with the specific thing it stands for, or cut it. Fix the pile-up, not every instance:
one "crucial" is fine. Do not swap one set of tics for another.

## 4. Check the reading level

- Measure as a check, not a target: average sentence length, the share of long words, and in
  English the Flesch reading ease and Flesch-Kincaid grade (formulas in the reference). Aim for an
  average of 15 to 20 words a sentence, and for the general public a grade of about 6 to 8 (reading
  ease 60 or more).
- Scores can be gamed by chopping sentences into fragments; short words in a confusing order still
  confuse. Read it aloud: wherever you stumble, the reader will too.
- Other languages: the same moves apply; the English formulas do not (Spanish and German have their
  own versions). Rely on sentence length and everyday words.

## 5. Keep the meaning

Compare every changed sentence with the original:

- The same facts and numbers, the same scope ("all" stays all, "some" stays some).
- The same conditions and exceptions (if, unless, only, except): these are the first to fall out.
- The same strength of obligation: "must" never becomes "should"; "may" never becomes "will".
- The same certainty: hedges that carry real doubt stay, in one plain word ("probably").
- In legal, medical, financial or safety text, mark every place where plain wording might change
  what the text commits to, and leave the decision to the person, or their adviser.

## 6. Deliver

- The rewritten text in the same format and order, unless you were asked to restructure.
- A short list of the changes that matter: anything that touched meaning, terms you explained or
  kept, what you cut and why, questions you could not settle.
- When it helps, the before and after numbers: "average sentence 31 → 16 words; grade 14 → 8".
- For a long document, rewrite one section first and agree the level before doing the rest.

## Pitfalls

- Dumbing down: lost precision, or a talking-down tone ("Simply…", "Just…").
- Meaning changed without notice: a softened obligation, a dropped exception.
- Choppy fragments written to please a readability score.
- Jargon replaced with something vaguer instead of something plainer.
- Polishing words when the real problem is the order of ideas.
- All the person's own voice removed along with the clutter.
