# Memos, proposals and how-to documents

Open this for a document that asks someone to decide (a decision memo, a proposal, a business case)
or one that helps someone do something (a README, a how-to guide).

## A memo or proposal that asks for a decision

The reader should be able to question the reasoning and decide from the page alone.

1. **The ask:** what is to be decided, by whom and by when, and the recommendation in a sentence or two.
2. **Why now:** who has the problem, how big it is (numbers with their base and period), and what
   happens if nothing changes.
3. **The options,** keeping things as they are among them: what each would change and what it costs
   or risks. A small table helps; then say why the recommended one wins.
4. **What it takes:** money, people, time, what gets dropped to make room, who owns it, how easy it is
   to undo. Real risks stay in the body, not in an appendix.
5. **The next checkpoint:** the first step, its owner and date, how success will be measured, and what
   would stop or change the plan.

- Length follows the substance: a page for one choice that is easy to undo; more only when the reader
  needs the evidence. A page limit is a ceiling, not a target.
- Use paragraphs where reasoning links evidence to a consequence; bullets for lists, not for arguments.
- Mark what is fact, estimate or assumption. Missing figures stay visibly missing
  (`[to check: how many customers were asked]`); never fill them in.
- Keep the evidence against the recommendation, and what it might mean. When the case is weak, write
  a conditional recommendation or propose a small test with a review date; making it sound certain
  helps nobody.
- Helping someone weigh options for their own choice is the decision-brief skill.

**A new product or service** can be tested by writing its future announcement first: one page in the
customer's words (who it is for, what they can now do, how to start, what it costs), then the
questions customers would ask (limits, price, trust) and the questions the team must answer
(evidence of demand, feasibility, cost, risks, the smallest test that could prove it wrong). Mark
imagined quotes and dates as imagined.

## A README

The front page of a project, in this order:

1. Its name, and one line on what it does and who it is for.
2. A quick start: the fewest steps from nothing to a visible result (install, run, see it work),
   ready to copy and paste, with defaults that need no setup. Run every command before publishing it.
3. Other ways to install, in order of preference.
4. Settings: each one, its default, and an example.
5. Common tasks, each with an example.
6. Where to get help, how to contribute, and the licence: a line each, with links.

Say what it does and how to use it, not how it works inside. Every page written has to be kept up
to date, so document less, but completely.

## A how-to guide

For someone who wants to do one thing, now:

- A title that names the goal: "Turn on two-step sign-in", not "Security".
- What they need first: an account, a version, permissions, how long it takes.
- Numbered steps, one action each, in the order they are done, with the screen's exact labels in
  bold (**Settings → Security**) or the exact command.
- After the steps that matter, what they should see; at the end, how they know it worked.
- A short "If it goes wrong" with the usual failures and their fixes.
- No background in the middle of the steps: link to an explanation instead.
- Test it by following it from a clean start, or watch someone who has never done the task follow it.
