# Solo business

On their own, one person has to find customers, do the work, answer them and keep the books, and
still have a life. So start from what they need the business to give them, test one small offer with
people who pay before building much, set prices that pay for all their hours, and check that the work
fits the week. Write each decision down with the result that would mean go on, change or stop. Staying
small, getting help, or going back to a job can all be the right outcome.

## 1. What they need from it

Ask one question at a time (the clarify-first skill's way), or use `ask_form` for several at once:

- **Income:** how much a month they need from it, and by when: pocket money, a second income, or a
  salary replaced.
- **Hours:** the hours a week they can really give it, after work, family and rest.
- **Risk:** the money they can afford to lose on it, and how many months they could manage without
  income from it.
- **Reach and skills:** people they can already reach (past clients, colleagues, a following, a local
  group), and what they are good at.
- **No-gos:** work, hours or risks they will not take on.

These are their limits: every later choice is checked against them. Mark what is unknown instead of
guessing, and do not assume they want investors, fast growth or "passive income".

## 2. Choose the kind of business

| Kind | What limits one person | The first real proof |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Freelance or made-to-order service | hours for selling and for the work; one big client | a paid job delivered in the hours planned |
| Fixed package (set scope, set price) | clients asking for more; time spent checking the work | the same package delivered several times at the planned cost |
| Digital product or software | finding buyers, support, keeping it working | people paying, using it, and paying again |
| Courses, content or a shop | building an audience, stock and delivery, platform fees, refunds | sales that hold after refunds and fees |

A service brings money in soonest but is capped by their hours; a product takes longer and still needs
selling and support. None is passive. A service can pay for a product later, but five clients are not
five people with the same need: look for the request that repeats.

## 3. Pick one offer and test it with paying customers

- **The offer in one sentence:** "For [who], I [do what] so that [result], for [price], within [time]."
  One group they can reach, one problem that costs those people money, time or worry, one result. For
  ideas, use the brainstorming skill; to choose between offers, decision-brief.
- **Check the problem is real:** what those people do about it now, who would pay, how urgently, and how
  the person would reach them.
- **Evidence, weakest to strongest:** they said they like it; you saw them struggle with a makeshift
  fix; they committed (booked a call, paid a deposit); they paid; it was delivered and they were
  pleased; they paid again. Compliments, likes and a waiting list are interest, not demand, and a
  refundable deposit may have to go back.
- **Test before building:** do the work by hand for a few paying customers, sell before making (a
  pre-sale), or offer a small fixed-price job. Write down first: what is and is not included, the
  price, the most time and money the test may take, the end date, and the result that means go on,
  change or stop ("3 of the 20 people I contact pay €150 by 30 November"). The threshold is their choice,
  set before the test, not a rule.
- Draft the messages, the offer page or the posts (the email and social-media skills); they do the
  sending and publishing, or say you may.

## 4. Price it so their hours pay

1. **Delivery hours:** from their hours a week, take off selling, admin and accounts, answering
   customers, learning, and a reserve for what goes wrong. What is left is the time they can sell.
2. **The floor:** (the income they need + business costs + tax set aside) ÷ delivery hours, all for the
   same week or month with time off counted, = the least an hour of delivery must earn.
3. **Per sale:** price − that sale's own costs (materials, payment and platform fees, tools paid per use,
   help bought in) = what the sale brings in. If it is below zero, more sales make the loss bigger:
   change the price or the offer.
4. **Does it fit the week?** (The sales that fit in the delivery hours × what each brings in) − fixed
   costs = what is left for them. Compare it with their income goal, not just with breaking even.
5. **The market:** what others charge for something similar (the research skill). Below the floor is
   fine only as a deliberate test with a limit.

A worked example: 12 hours a week, of which 3 for selling, 1 for admin and 2 for support and reserve
leave 6 for delivery. A report takes 3 hours, so 2 a week fit. Each sells for €400 and costs €40:
€360 each. They want €1,000 a week for themselves and have €100 a week of costs: 1,100 ÷ 360 = 3.06,
so 4 reports, twice what fits. Raise the price, change the offer, lower the goal or find more hours;
never plan 4 reports into 6 hours. More cases are in `references/decision-record.md`.

- **Money comes late:** invoices are often paid 30 to 60 days later, so ask for a deposit or payment up
  front where that is normal. A year paid in advance is work still owed, not money to spend.
- **Tax, registration, insurance, invoicing rules:** the official guidance where they live, or an
  accountant. For a monthly forecast, a loan or an investor, follow the financial-model skill.

## 5. Fit the work to one person

- **One offer and one way to find customers at a time:** people they know and referrals, partners, a
  marketplace, search, or an audience they already have. Keep hours for selling every week, busy or not.
- **A limit on work at once:** how many clients or orders they can take before quality slips.
- **Promises they can keep:** reply times, support hours, delivery dates. Promises already made stand
  until changed with the customer.
- **More work means a new price:** when a client asks for more than the offer, work out the new hours
  and quote for them, or offer the standard package.
- **Time off:** someone else can get into the billing, the website and the backups, a message for
  customers is ready, and sales can be paused.
- **Automation and help:** first drop work nobody needs, then make the rest the same each time, then
  compare doing it by hand, automating it or paying someone, counting setup, checking, fixing and cost.
  Prices, refunds and deadlines stay theirs: no bot makes those promises. In Hydor, a job that repeats
  can become an app or a scheduled task (the app-builder skill).
- **Most of the money from one client or one platform** is a risk: name it, and plan a second source.

## 6. Say no, change or stop

- **Say no** to work outside the offer, below the floor, beyond their hours, or with risks they cannot
  afford.
- **Change or stop** when sales lose money, the money set aside for the test is spent, the work does not
  fit the hours, quality keeps slipping, or the end date passes without the agreed result. Never fix it
  by quietly working more hours.
- Pausing, changing the offer, getting help, hiring, going back to a job and closing are all fair
  outcomes. When it is close, weigh them with the decision-brief skill.

## 7. Write the decision down, and check it

One page; the form and filled-in examples are in `references/decision-record.md`: the date and the
decision; their limits; the offer; the evidence so far and what is unknown; the numbers (price, costs
per sale, hours, how many sales fit, against the income goal); the main bottleneck; the next test with
its limits and date; what means go on, change or stop; and when to look again.

- Keep it with `save_doc` to read and print, or `save_sheet` when they want to change the numbers
  themselves (a Markdown file otherwise).
- Check before handing it over: the hours add up to their total; every figure is theirs or marked as an
  estimate; the sums are right (work them out, do not eyeball them); the test has a date and a stop rule.
- Offer a review on the test's end date with `schedule_task` (they confirm it on a card).
- When the offer, the price or the hours change, work the numbers out again before saying yes to more.

## Limits

This is planning help, not legal, tax or financial advice: rules on registration, contracts, tax and
insurance differ by country, so point to the official guidance or an accountant. Do not contact
customers, publish, sign up for services or take payments for them without their go-ahead.

## Pitfalls

- Building for months before anyone has paid.
- Compliments or a waiting list taken as demand.
- A price that pays only for the delivery hours.
- A sale that loses money, kept in the hope that volume fixes it.
- Extra work taken on at the old price.
- A plan that needs more hours than they have.
- Most of the money from one client or one platform.
- Automation counted as free time, with no setup or checking.
- Plans built on famous success stories instead of their own numbers.
